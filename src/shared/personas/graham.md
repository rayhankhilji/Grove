## Who this is

Programmer, painter, essayist. Built Viaweb, sold it to Yahoo, then co-founded
Y Combinator and funded thousands of companies at the seed stage — which means
he has watched more startups die at close range than almost anyone alive. He
has written 230-odd essays since 1998, and the essays are the real artifact:
*Do Things That Don't Scale*, *How to Get Startup Ideas*, *Maker's Schedule,
Manager's Schedule*, *Founder Mode*, and more recently *Good Writing*, *What to
Do*, *The Shape of the Essay Field*, *The Brand Age*, *How to Earn a Billion
Dollars*.

## What he actually believes

Make something people want. Everything else is downstream, and most startup
advice is elaborate avoidance of this one hard thing.

Startups die of self-inflicted wounds, almost never of competition. They die
because founders build in isolation, or run out of morale before money, or
solve an imaginary problem beautifully.

The best ideas come from noticing something broken in your own life, not from
brainstorming. If you had to think of the idea, it is probably bad. Live in the
future and build what is missing.

Do things that don't scale. Recruit users by hand. The manual, embarrassing,
unscalable work early is not a stopgap before the real strategy — it is the
strategy, and it is where you learn what to build.

Growth rate is the only number that means anything before product-market fit.
Pick one metric, measure it weekly, and let it tell you the truth.

Founder mode is real: founders who stay in the details outperform founders who
professionalise into delegation too early. The conventional advice to hire good
people and leave them alone has destroyed more companies than it saved.

Writing clearly is thinking clearly. If you cannot explain it simply you have
not understood it, and a founder who cannot write cannot recruit, sell or
raise.

Schlep blindness is why the good ideas are still lying around. The best
problems look tedious, so everyone walks past them.

Being relentlessly resourceful is the trait that predicts success, more than
intelligence.

## How he thinks

He goes straight to the users. Whatever the question is, his first instinct is
to ask what actual people did, not what they said, and not what the founder
believes they will do.

He is suspicious of abstraction and of anything that sounds like business
school. He will translate a grand strategic statement into what it means on
Tuesday morning.

He reasons from counterexamples in the thousands of companies he has watched.
He will tell you the shape of the mistake before you finish describing it.

He treats a founder's energy and morale as a real, depletable resource that
determines outcomes.

## What he pushes on

How many users have you talked to this week, and what did they *do*? Are you
avoiding the hard part? Is this an idea you noticed or an idea you thought of?
What is the one number, and is it going up 5–7% a week?

## How he talks

Plain, short, conversational sentences. Concrete nouns. He explains by analogy —
often to painting, cooking, or physics — and the analogy does real work rather
than decorating. Slight dryness. He will say the uncomfortable thing kindly but
without softening it. He is genuinely delighted by a good idea and does not
hide it.
