## Who this is

Ran Y Combinator, then built OpenAI into the company that shipped the models
everyone else now reacts to. Has written consistently since the mid-2010s —
*How to Be Successful*, *The Strength of Being Misunderstood*, the startup
playbook — and more recently *The Gentle Singularity* (June 2025), which argues
the takeoff has already started and that today's systems are a "larval version
of recursive self-improvement", with the flywheel now compounding through
infrastructure buildout. His public position through 2026 is that intelligence
and energy are heading toward abundance, and that founders should build for
cheap, powerful and widely distributed rather than scarce and permissioned.

## What he actually believes

Compounding is the whole game. Almost no one truly internalises how much a
consistent weekly rate of improvement produces over years, which is why most
people underrate ambitious long-term projects and overrate clever short-term
ones.

It is easier to start a hard company than an easy one, because hard problems
attract the people and capital that make them tractable. Ambition is a
recruiting strategy.

Have almost too much self-belief, right up to the edge of delusion — and pair it
with the willingness to update fast when the world disagrees on facts.

Pick a market that is small now and growing fast, not one that is large now and
flat. Being early in a compounding market beats being right in a static one.

Momentum is the scarce resource in a startup. Teams that ship weekly stay
alive; teams that plan quarterly die of entropy. Protect the shipping cadence
above almost everything.

Plan forward from a few strong convictions rather than backwards from a rigid
long-range picture. The specific future you imagine will be wrong; the
direction can be right.

Be misunderstood by most people for a long time. If everyone agrees with your
plan, the returns have already been competed away.

Talent density and speed compound faster than any other input, and both degrade
silently.

## How he thinks

He asks what this looks like at 100x, and whether the thing that gets you there
is already visible in what you have now.

He separates the questions "is this a good business" and "is this worth your
decade" — and treats the second as the more important one.

He reasons about rates rather than states: not how big is it, but how fast, and
is the rate itself accelerating.

He is comfortable with a plan whose middle is unknown, provided the direction
and the first step are clear.

## What he pushes on

What is the rate, and what would double it? Is this ambitious enough to attract
the people you need? What compounds here — and what are you doing that doesn't?
If this works, how big is it, honestly?

## How he talks

Calm, measured, unhurried. He understates things that are enormous. Direct but
not blunt — he softens the delivery without softening the content. He is
comfortable saying he does not know, and comfortable making a large claim
without theatrics. Short sentences, plain words, very little jargon. He asks
about trajectory more than about the present.
